Why Is My Eye Red?

Getting to the Cause of a Red, Irritated Eye in Pine Beach

Book AppointmentCall: (732) 978-9306

A red eye can be a small annoyance or a sign that something needs attention soon. Maybe it’s been pink and scratchy for days, or maybe you glanced in the mirror and spotted a bright red patch that wasn’t there this morning. The color alone doesn’t tell you much—the cause does. Red eyes are part of Dr. Marc Ullman’s clinical focus at Academy Vision, and getting to the cause is where real relief starts.

Common Reasons an Eye Turns Red

  • Dry eye. When tears don’t keep the surface moist, eyes can look red and feel gritty, especially after screen time. More on dry eye
  • Allergies. Itchy, watery, puffy eyes, usually both at once and often with the seasons.
  • Pink eye. Redness with discharge or crusty lashes can be conjunctivitis. More on pink eye
  • A broken blood vessel. A flat, bright red patch on the white of the eye that looks alarming but is usually painless and fades over a couple of weeks.
  • Contact lens trouble. Wearing lenses too long, sleeping in them or an infection under a lens.
  • Something in the eye, or a scratch. What to do when something gets in your eye
  • Inflammation inside the eye or a sudden rise in eye pressure. Less common, usually painful, and in need of prompt care.

Mild Irritation or Something More?

Often mild: redness without real pain, clear vision, and eyes that settle with rest, a break from screens or lubricating drops.

Call an eye doctor promptly: pain rather than scratchiness, sensitivity to light, blurry vision that doesn’t clear when you blink, thick discharge, redness after an injury, any red eye in a contact lens wearer, or redness that keeps coming back.

Go to the nearest emergency room right away: sudden vision loss, a chemical splash, an injury that cuts or punctures the eye, a curtain or shadow over your vision, flashes of light with a sudden shower of new floaters, or severe eye pain with a headache, nausea or halos around lights.

What a Red-Eye Exam Usually Covers

A visit for a red eye has three aims: finding the cause, easing the discomfort and judging how urgent it is. It usually includes:

  • Questions about when the redness started, whether it hurts, any discharge, allergies, recent colds and contact lens habits
  • A check of your vision
  • A magnified look at the lids, the white of the eye and the clear front surface
  • A special dye and blue light to reveal scratches or sores on the surface
  • When needed, a pressure check and a look inside the eye

Why Drugstore Drops and the ER Aren’t Always the Answer

Whitening drops can hide redness for a few hours, but they don’t treat the cause, and frequent use can leave eyes redder when they wear off. A retail counter can sell you drops or a new box of lenses, but it isn’t set up to examine an inflamed eye. The emergency room is the right call for the warning signs above. For most other cases, an eye doctor is in a better position to examine the eye closely and treat what’s behind it. Our emergency eye care page covers other urgent problems.

Should I go to the emergency room for a red eye?

Go right away for sudden vision loss, a chemical splash, a cut or puncture, a curtain or shadow over your vision, flashes with a sudden shower of new floaters, or severe pain with a headache, nausea or halos. For a red eye without those signs, start by calling an eye doctor.

I wear contacts and my eye is red. What should I do?

Take your lenses out, leave them out and switch to your glasses. A red eye in a contact lens wearer, especially with pain or light sensitivity, can be an infection of the cornea that needs prompt care, so call an eye doctor and bring your lenses and case along. If lenses often leave your eyes red, a contact lens exam can help you find a more comfortable fit.

Is a bright red spot on the white of my eye serious?

Usually not. A broken blood vessel under the surface can follow a cough, a sneeze or heavy lifting, and it typically fades on its own. Have it checked if it hurts, affects your vision, follows an injury or keeps happening.
