A Quiet Condition, Watched Closely

Glaucoma Monitoring and Management in Pine Beach

Book AppointmentCall: (732) 978-9306

Glaucoma usually moves slowly and quietly. Most people feel completely normal while it develops, and the earliest changes only show up during a careful eye exam. That’s why it calls for a steady, long-term watch rather than a one-time check.

We keep a close watch on glaucoma and help manage the condition to protect your vision. Whether you’ve just been told you’re at risk or you’ve been living with glaucoma for years, our optometrists take the time to listen, explain, and make sure you feel comfortable.

How Glaucoma Affects the Optic Nerve

The optic nerve is the bundle of fibers that carries everything your eye sees to your brain. Glaucoma damages those fibers, most often when the pressure inside the eye is more than the nerve can tolerate, although it can also happen when pressure readings fall in the normal range.

In the most common form, the loss begins at the edges of your vision and creeps inward so gradually that your brain fills in the gaps. Many people notice nothing until a large share of the nerve is affected. Vision lost to glaucoma can’t be brought back, but finding it early and treating it can slow or stop further loss.

Three Reasons Regular Glaucoma Checks Matter

  • Early detection. Routine exams can pick up changes in the optic nerve long before you would notice anything yourself.
  • A baseline that’s yours. Your first thorough set of measurements becomes the reference point for every visit after it.
  • A plan that adjusts. If your readings shift, your follow-up schedule or treatment can change with them, instead of waiting for symptoms.

Early detection and monitoring of glaucoma is one of Dr. Tyler Lesko’s clinical specialties. Dr. Marc Ullman takes pride in managing chronic eye disease.

What a Glaucoma Check Looks At

No single reading tells the whole story, so glaucoma testing usually brings a few pieces together:

  • Eye pressure. A reading of each eye’s internal pressure.
  • The optic nerve. A close look at the nerve’s shape and color for early signs of damage.
  • Related tests. When they’re needed, checks of your side vision, the thickness of your cornea, or the eye’s drainage angle fill in the details.

We’ll talk through anything we find in plain language, so you understand where things stand.

Watching for Change, Year After Year

With glaucoma, the trend matters more than any single number. A pressure reading or a nerve measurement means the most when it sits beside the same measurement from previous years, so even a small shift stands out.

That comparison gets harder when your results are scattered across different offices. Each one may use different instruments and methods, and no one has the full picture in a single chart. Keeping your glaucoma care in one place lets every visit build on the last. Families from Pine Beach, Beachwood, and Toms River come back to us year after year, and with a condition like this, that continuity counts.

Part of Your Medical Eye Care

Glaucoma is one of the conditions we manage as part of our medical eye care. If you live with diabetes, read how diabetic eye exams watch over the retina, and learn about macular degeneration and cataract care, two more conditions that become more common with age.

Will I notice symptoms if I have glaucoma?

Usually not at first. The most common type causes no pain and no early change you can see on your own, which is why regular exams matter. A less common type, angle-closure glaucoma, can come on suddenly with a painful red eye, blurred vision, halos around lights, headache, or nausea. That is an emergency, so seek care immediately.

Who has a higher risk of glaucoma?

Anyone can develop glaucoma, but the risk rises with age and is higher if a parent, brother, or sister has it. Your odds are also higher if you have high eye pressure or diabetes, are very nearsighted, have used steroid medicines for a long time, have had a serious eye injury, or are of Asian, African, or Hispanic descent.

How is glaucoma treated?

Treatment aims to lower the pressure inside the eye. Prescription eye drops, used daily, are the most common first step. Laser treatment is another option, sometimes chosen first, and surgery may be considered when other treatments aren’t enough. Whatever the approach, steady follow-up shows whether it’s working, and we’ll help you manage the condition over the long term.

How often should I be checked?

It depends on your age, your risk factors, and whether you’ve already been diagnosed. Adults without risk factors are generally advised to have a complete eye exam by age 40, and people with glaucoma or a higher risk are usually seen more often. The right interval is the one built around your own readings, so ask at your next visit.
